# Proposal Power-Paragraph Method

Parent skill: [Premium Commercial Writing](../SKILL.md). Companion bank: [proposal phrase bank](proposal-phrase-bank.md).

**When to read:** before drafting any proposal, EOI, letter proposal, retainer proposal, partnership proposal or executive summary, and again when a draft reads as generic. The method gives every scored section a short, pre-agreed lead paragraph so that an evaluator who only skims still takes away the right message.

Method source: the idea of a short lead paragraph with fixed sentence jobs, written for each evaluation point before drafting, is adapted from Debelak, D. (2006) *Perfect Phrases for Business Proposals and Business Plans*, McGraw-Hill. The sheet structure, section mapping, engagement-shape table, document outlines, tone rules and East African adaptation below are this engine's own and follow the engine's pipeline, not the book.

## 1. Why the method exists

- Evaluators skim and remember only a few phrases. Choose those phrases deliberately.
- Every evaluator scores against criteria, published or not. Each criterion deserves one lead paragraph that makes its point unmistakably.
- Bids are won by stating the client's own view of its need and goal, then proving fit, visible tailoring, deliverables and price linked to that goal, and value that outlasts the project.

## 2. The power paragraph

A power paragraph is three or four sentences, each with one job, in this order:

| Sentence | Job | Test |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | State the fact the reader cares about (need, goal, requirement or price) | A named party is the subject; a number, place or date appears in sentence 1 or 2 |
| 2 | Show why it matters now (consequence or cost of doing nothing) | The reader can see what is lost if nothing changes |
| 3 (optional) | Add the emphasis the engagement shape demands (see section 4) | The sentence would not fit a different engagement unchanged |
| 4 | State the forward implication the reader should accept | Looks outward to the client's position; does not repeat sentence 1 |

Working length: 45 to 110 words; each sentence about 25 words or fewer.

## 3. The power-paragraph sheet, in pipeline order

Before drafting sections, write one lead paragraph for each evaluation point below. The rows follow this engine's pipeline so each paragraph lands where it is used. Together they form the **power-paragraph sheet**, the message map for the bid.

| Lead paragraph | Sentence jobs | Leads section |
|---|---|---|
| **Need** (as the client sees it) | Need in the client's words, with source → what solving it achieves now and what doing nothing costs → longer-term benefit | `03-understanding-of-assignment`; opening of `02-executive-summary` |
| **Goal** | The client's goal → why it is the right goal → why it is achievable, with evidence | `03`; objectives in `06-methodology` |
| **Fit** (expertise) | What the work demands → our matching people and methods → summary of comparable work (full list in an annex) | `04-firm-profile`, `05-relevant-experience`, `07-team-composition` |
| **Tailoring** | What is special about this client's circumstances → how this offer is built around them → why that gives the best route | `06-methodology`; differentiators in `02` |
| **Deliverables** | What the client asked for → what we add → how the deliverables reach the goal | `06`; deliverables table in `08-work-plan` |
| **Price and value** | The price, stated plainly → what the client achieves for it → what is included beyond that → why fit makes price secondary | Opening of `10-financial-proposal`; close of `02` |
| **Value beyond the project** | Benefit to the whole organisation six months to three years out → why it matters → the opportunity it opens | Close of `02`; close of `01-cover-letter` |

**Sheet procedure**

1. Capture the client's words for needs and goals from discovery, clarification meetings and the ToR; record each source (meeting and date, or clause).
2. Identify the engagement shape (section 4) and the published evaluation criteria.
3. Map each lead paragraph to a criterion; in scored procurements (PPDA, PPRA, World Bank, GIZ and similar grids) open with the evaluator's criterion wording.
4. Draft the paragraphs and run the checklist (section 7).
5. Where possible, agree the sheet with the proposal lead or client sponsor before drafting; it exposes weak proof early.
6. Place each paragraph as the opening of its section. Reuse the key phrase, not the whole paragraph, in the executive summary and cover letter.

## 4. Engagement shape changes the emphasis

Decide the engagement shape by asking two questions: how closely will the client work alongside us, and how many organisations share the outcome? The answer sets sentence 3's emphasis in every lead paragraph.

| Client involvement | Parties | Emphasis to carry | Opening-angle slot |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low: we deliver an independent product (study, audit) | One client | Independence, completeness, quality of the artefact the client will act on | "[Firm] will independently [assess] [topic] and deliver [artefact] that [Client] can act on without further consultancy." |
| High: we deliver with client staff (roll-out, process change) | One client | Joint working, skills transfer, named people on site, training | "[Firm] will work alongside [Client]'s [team], transferring [skill] so that [team] runs [system] independently by [date]." |
| Continuing: we take on a function (retainer, managed service) | One client | Reliability, service levels, turnaround, relief of a recurring burden | "From [date], [Firm] will take responsibility for [function] and meet [service level] every [period]." |
| Shared: organisations cooperate for mutual benefit | Two or more | Each party's need given equal weight; cost split; combined reach | "[Party A] and [Party B] each need [need], and each holds what the other lacks: [asset A] and [asset B]." |
| Contractual: a formal joint venture or partnership | Two or more | Roles, responsibilities, how the parties work together, revenue or profit split | "[Party A] and [Party B] propose to [objective], with [A] responsible for [role] and [B] for [role], sharing [revenue] [x/y]." |

## 5. Short or full document

Ask the client which format it prefers; some want a short letter first and a full proposal later. A procurement template always overrides this section.

**Short letter proposal (two to three pages).** Compress the pipeline into one document in this order: purpose line → need → goal → approach with one comparable success → action plan (attached if long) → deliverables → price (or cost split for cooperation deals) → value beyond the project and the next step.

**Full proposal (no template imposed).** Use the engine's standard section order in the [proposal router](../../../SKILL.md): cover letter, executive summary (purpose, need, goal, approach, fit, price and value), understanding of the assignment (including options considered, which shows judgement and often leads to follow-on work), firm profile and relevant experience with references, methodology (targets, components, governance, meeting and reporting cadence, each party's resources and responsibilities), team (one paragraph per person, CVs annexed), work plan, and a separate financial proposal with guarantees and the cost-benefit case.

## 6. Tone rules for premium, human, professional output

| Rule | Apply it like this |
|---|---|
| T1 Specific over superlative | Replace "best", "ideal", "perfect", "world-class" with the evidence that would justify them. At most one calibrated superlative per section, and only with proof. |
| T2 The client's words beat ours | In needs and goals sections, paraphrase-quote the client: "In our meeting on [date], your finance director described month-end as 'a week we lose every month'." |
| T3 Voice follows format | Third person ("[Firm]", "The Consultant") for formal procurement; first-person plural for letter proposals, cover letters and retainers; follow the loaded profile. |
| T4 One idea per sentence | About 25 words or fewer. A power paragraph must survive a skim. |
| T5 Reassure, do not boast | Feasibility sentences sound calm and precedent-based: "has done this at four comparable sites". |
| T6 Conclusions look forward and outward | Close on the client's future position, never "In conclusion, we have shown…". |
| T7 Never apologise for price, never bury it | Price first in the cost paragraph; value beside it; no "only", "just", "a small investment of". |
| T8 East African professional register | Measured and courteous; no US hype idioms ("game-changer", "slam dunk", "can't-miss"); UGX/KES/TZS with thousand separators; name the district or town. |
| T9 No machine-made tells | Apply the banned vocabulary in [anti-ai-slop](../../../meta/anti-ai-slop/SKILL.md). Plain, concrete sentences are the remedy. |

**Grammar that makes the style work**

1. Named-subject declaratives: "[Client] is considering…", "[Firm] will deliver…". Any sentence can then be lifted into another document without losing meaning.
2. Numbers early: counts, currency, percentages and time in the first or second clause.
3. Cause and consequence chains: "[condition], which is [effect] on [thing that matters]".
4. Named alternatives: say what the client does today ("re-keys every invoice into the portal").
5. Ownership verbs for the client's desired state: cut, shorten, recover, free up, secure, expand, establish, protect.
6. Conditional mood for benefits we cannot guarantee ("would lift"); indicative for facts.

## 7. Power-paragraph quality checklist

- [ ] One job per sentence, in the prescribed order.
- [ ] A named party is the grammatical subject of sentence 1.
- [ ] At least one number (count, currency, percentage or time) in sentence 1 or 2, sourced.
- [ ] Needs and goals use the client's perceived need, ideally in their words, with the source recorded.
- [ ] Sentence 2 states what doing nothing costs the client.
- [ ] Sentence 3, where used, carries the engagement-shape emphasis.
- [ ] The conclusion states a forward implication and does not repeat sentence 1.
- [ ] Every superlative ("only", "first", "largest") has evidence in the paragraph or the next one.
- [ ] 45 to 110 words.
- [ ] The key phrase reappears in the executive summary; the full paragraph is not copied.

## 8. Habits to remove

| Habit | Correction |
|---|---|
| Stating what we think the client needs | State what the client feels it needs, in its words, with the source |
| Assuming the client will act once the need is stated | Add the sentence that shows the cost of doing nothing |
| Quietly re-scoping when the goal is unreachable | Tell the client before submission and agree a revised goal |
| One generic proposal sent to many buyers | Visible customisation in nouns (their sites, staff, customers), never adjectives such as "tailored" or "bespoke" |
| Revealing reuse ("as we proposed to X") | Refer to comparable success ("this mirrors our work for X"), never to a recycled proposal |
| Listing what we *can* do when the goal cannot be met | Say so plainly and offer the achievable variant with its trade-off |
| Five differentiators | One or two decisive differentiators, each proved |
| Generic benefit nouns ("efficiency", "growth", "value") | Say efficiency *of what*, *by how much*, *by when* |
| Backward-looking conclusions | Close on the client's position six months to three years out |
| Long documents | Letter proposals of two to three pages; lead every section with its power paragraph |

## 9. Adapting the paragraph beyond proposals

| Channel | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Cover email or WhatsApp covering note | Needs → goal → fit → one next step: "[Observed situation]. If [goal], [benefit]. We helped [peer] do that in [time]. Could we take twenty minutes on [day]?" |
| Case study or past-performance card | Needs → goal → what we delivered → result against baseline → what it made possible next |
| Service page or capability statement | Needs paragraph in second person; expertise paragraph as "why us" |
| Orals opening | The need paragraph spoken in under 45 seconds; see [tender orals](../../../strategy-positioning/tender-orals-and-proposal-presentation/SKILL.md) |
