# Objection Root Causes and Closing Language

Parent skill: [Sales Discovery and Objection Handling](../SKILL.md). Related: [proposal objection handling](../../../profiles-sectors/references/proposal-objection-handling.md), [proposal phrase bank](../../../writing-content/premium-commercial-writing/references/proposal-phrase-bank.md).

**When to read:** when objections recur across bids, when preparing a pitch or clarification meeting, when a proposal needs a direct and courteous close, when debriefing a won or lost bid, or when building introductions and expert standing ahead of a pursuit.

Sources (methods only, paraphrased): root-cause diagnosis, stature, the convincing-presentation building blocks, proof rules and closing behaviours from Maltz, M., Kennedy, D. S., Brooks, W. T., Oechsli, M., Paul, J. and Yellen, P. (1998) *Zero-Resistance Selling*, Prentice Hall Press (trademarked programme names are not used); consultative sales steps from Nelson, J. (2019) *The Seven Figure Agency Roadmap*; follow-up discipline consistent with the engine's SaaS discovery references. Excluded from the source on ethical grounds: fear-based "prevention selling" beyond the facts, pushing buyers into debt, inflated stature, and all pseudo-scientific claims (extra-sensory influence, subliminal programming, health claims about emotions).

## 1. Diagnose objections at the root

If we are in front of the right buyer, recurring objections come from one of three upstream causes:

| Root cause | Signal | Fix upstream |
|---|---|---|
| Prospecting | Wrong or unqualified buyer (no budget, no authority, no real need, wrong timing) | Tighter qualification and bid/no-bid discipline |
| Positioning | The buyer does not see us as the expert for this problem | Earned stature: publications, talks, credentials, named comparable work, introductions |
| Presenting | The buyer did not see value, proof or fit | Diagnosis before prescription, customisation, proof near claims |

**Objection register (monthly or per bid):** objection wording → stage → root cause → current response → win rate → new response to test → owner. The aim is fewer objections, not better comebacks. If the same first move keeps producing the same objection, change the first move.

For each persistent objection, draft many possible answers and a set of questions; a well-chosen question is often stronger than a statement ("Compared with what?", "What would you need to see to be confident this pays for itself?").

**Objection or rejection:** an objection is a real concern that can be addressed; a rejection means no current fit. Record rejections honestly and do not chase them.

## 2. Pre-meeting presentation checklist

Before any pitch, walk-through or clarification meeting, confirm each item. The checklist is this engine's own; the underlying practices are attributed above.

| Check | What "ready" looks like |
|---|---|
| We have diagnosed before prescribing | The buyer's situation is written in their words, with the source of each point |
| The core story is adapted to this buyer | Their data, terms, sites and constraints appear in the material; nothing reads as a standard deck |
| Our main limitation is named | One sentence states it, why it exists and how it is covered: "In fairness, we do not [x]. Here is why, and here is how we cover it." |
| Every benefit is one the buyer values | Each point was tested with the buyer or is dropped; points they do not value add perceived cost |
| Both kinds of value are present | Measurable value (money, time, risk) and personal value (relief, confidence, standing with their board) |
| Claims have proof beside them | See section 3 |
| The picture is concrete | A before-and-after comparison, demonstration or specific scene: "Picture the front desk on Monday: patients booked on WhatsApp before they arrive." |

## 3. Claim-to-proof register

Keep one register per bid and apply these tests to every row:

- **Supportable:** if we cannot evidence a claim, remove or qualify it before submission.
- **Placed:** the evidence sits next to the claim, not in a distant annex only.
- **Dated:** each proof item carries a date; anything older than twelve months is re-checked or retired.
- **Trusted:** we use only evidence we know to be valid and would defend under questioning.
- **Sufficient:** where a claim is central to the award, attach more than one independent proof.

## 4. Price objections: show the value difference

Except for true commodities, buyers do not buy on lowest price alone. If price dominates, the difference has not been shown. Make it concrete (inputs, method, assurance, outcome, risk of failure), show what the cheaper choice costs later, and restate value against price. Language: "There are cheaper options. The question is what the cheaper option costs you when [failure scenario]." For strategic clients, move to the [strategic-account negotiation](../../premium-pricing-and-value-defense/references/strategic-account-negotiation.md) patterns.

## 5. Consultative sequence for private-sector pursuits

1. **Short first call:** a few questions, then offer to study their situation before recommending anything: "Rather than pitch now, let me review [their current set-up] and show you on [day] where you are strong, where you are losing [enquiries], and what we would do."
2. **Research before the meeting:** their current performance, gaps and the cost of those gaps in their terms.
3. **Review meeting:** rapport → goals ("If everything went to plan, where would you be in twelve months?") → findings, praising something genuine before naming gaps → solution narrative linking the mechanism to their outcome → two options.
4. **Ask for a decision:** check readiness ("On a scale of one to ten, how ready are you? What would make it a ten?"); if undecided, agree a decide-by date and book the proposal walk-through.

## 6. Closing language

Replace timid closes ("What do you think?", "Let us know", "Any thoughts?") with a clear, courteous request for one action.

- Choice close: "There are two sensible ways to start: [option A] or [option B]. Which suits you better?"
- Organised close: "Here is how we would start: engagement letter this week, discovery on [date], first draft by [date]."
- Group session close: "Before you leave today: [step 1], [step 2], [step 3]. [Name] has the forms."
- Reassurance for a hesitant buyer: "It is sensible to pause before a decision like this. Here is what protects you: [stage gate / guarantee / exit point]."
- Permission to meet professionally (for someone known socially): "Would you have any objection to us meeting on a professional basis, so I can show you what we have done for [peer type]?"

Confident is not pushy: let the conversation move at its own pace, match the right client to the right service, and accept a "no" without taking it personally. Wanting to be liked more than wanting a clear decision is the most common closing failure.

## 7. Go-dark discipline and follow-up

When the buyer goes quiet, do not send "just checking in". Each follow-up carries something useful: a relevant insight, a regulatory or market signal, an answer to an open question, or a proposal for a decision date. Re-engage through the sponsor route agreed during discovery, not through pressure.

## 8. Win-loss debrief (after every significant bid)

**Lost bid:** request the formal debrief where the procurement rules allow it (for example the debriefing rights under the PPDA or PPRA regimes; verify the current procedure) and ask informally:

"Thank you for considering us. I am not calling to reopen the decision; I want to improve. Could you give me fifteen minutes of honest feedback on where our proposal fell short for you?"

Questions: What made the winning option feel safer? What did we say that made you doubt us? What did we leave unanswered? What would you change first if you were advising us?

**Won bid:** ask what tipped the decision and which part of the proposal the evaluators quoted back.

**Record and learn:** log the scores by criterion, the cause and the change to make, using temporary and specific explanations ("our methodology scored six points lower because the method diagram was missing") rather than permanent ones ("they only buy on price"). Feed the change into the relevant skill through the Kaizen cycle.

## 9. Expert standing and introductions (ethical)

- **Earned stature only:** publications, talks at association or professional-body events, certifications, and named comparable work. Never inflate credentials or imply endorsements.
- **Host introduction script:** "[Name] leads [firm], which has [distinct achievement with number]. [She / he] wrote [publication] and has advised [recognisable client types]. Today [she / he] will show you [specific outcome]."
- **Introductions beat cold approaches:** "I wonder if you could help me with something. I am trying to find the right person to speak to about [topic]."
- **Give first:** make useful introductions between others and share useful information without an agenda.
- **Procurement boundary:** do not network with evaluators or officials in ways that breach conflict-of-interest or tender-communication rules; relationship-building stops at the procurement's blackout rules.

## 10. Preparing for a tough meeting

Write every question a sceptical buyer could ask, in its hardest form, and draft calm answers. Rehearse aloud. Remember that a demanding buyer is one who can tell experts from pretenders, which favours real expertise. For formal orals use the [tender orals skill](../../tender-orals-and-proposal-presentation/SKILL.md).
