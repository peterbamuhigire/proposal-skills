# Orals Preparation and Room Readiness

Parent skill: [Tender Orals and Proposal Presentation](../SKILL.md).

**When to read:** at workflow steps 2, 3 and 8, when planning the presentation, storyboarding it and preparing the room, equipment and handouts. Also useful for client workshops, inception meetings and steering committees that the proposal promises to run.

**Sources:** organised as this engine's orals countdown (brief → build → materials → room → delivery → meetings the proposal promises). It synthesises general presentation and meeting practice, including Kupsh, J. and Graves, P. R. (1993) *How to Create High-Impact Business Presentations*, NTC Business Books, Minto's top-down structuring (see the pyramid guidance in [proposal strategy and persuasion](../../../profiles-sectors/references/proposal-strategy-and-persuasion.md)), and the engine's own East African field practice. Obsolete media advice, 1990s figures for rooms, lighting, sound and climate, and unsupported claims about visual learning and colour effects are excluded; venue facts are confirmed with the venue, current standards and local fire limits.

## 1. Brief: the presentation strategy sheet (about ten working days out)

Complete the sheet before anyone opens slide software.

| Heading | Record |
|---|---|
| Rules | Duration, attendees allowed, recording, language, whether slides must be submitted in advance |
| Panel | Names, roles, who decides, what they already know, terms and acronyms they use, why they attend, headcount |
| Slot | Date, start and end, position in the day (first, after lunch, last), other bidders or speakers |
| Objective | What the panel should believe or decide by the end, framed as the panel's problems, and how we will know it worked |
| Venue | Address, travel time, room, fixed or movable seating, stage or floor |
| Equipment | Screen and aspect ratio, display inputs, adapters, clicker, microphone type, sound for video, internet, **power backup** |
| Materials | Handouts (quantity, timing), samples, flip chart, printing access |
| Contacts | Organiser, venue technician, the host who will introduce the team (send the introduction text) |

Objective statement pattern: "By the end of this [n]-minute presentation, the panel will [believe / decide] [outcome], because we will show [evidence 1, 2, 3]."

East African planning points: offline deck and charged devices with a power bank or small UPS for power cuts and weak connectivity; time for opening protocol (recognising dignitaries by precedence, sometimes a prayer) and late starts, with a flexible opening and a hard close; the agreed language mix; the hierarchy in the room on government and development-partner panels.

## 2. Build: storyboard from the top down (about seven working days out)

1. Write the governing idea and the three or four supporting points first (top-down structure), mapped to the scoring criteria.
2. Break each supporting point into cards, one point per card, and choose its form: a short statement, a number or chart, a diagram (organisation chart, map, process flow, timeline) or an image.
3. Link each card to the proposal section it summarises; nothing new enters that is not in the submission.
4. Write the opening (client situation and governing idea) and the close (value beyond the project and the requested next step) once the body is fixed.
5. Signpost the structure aloud: "Three things will decide whether [outcome]. We will take them in turn."

People remember what is new to them, relevant to them, emphasised and clearly shown; design every card for that.

**Slide discipline (the design engine governs type, colour and layout):** one message per slide, written as a short statement; supporting keywords, not paragraphs; parallel wording in lists; acronyms spelled out unless everyone knows them; mixed case rather than capitals; one dominant element; generous empty space; a title slide on screen as people enter. Persistent elements (title, logo, section marker) sit on the master; route visual design to `design-system-skills`.

**Honest charts:** the same axis scale across related charts, labelled axes, no decorative effects that distort, and detailed figures moved to the handout.

## 3. Materials: handouts and leave-behinds (about three working days out)

- Decide when the panel receives material: at the start if they must refer to it (then say when to look and when to listen); at the end if it would distract. Senior officials often value a one-page summary more than a deck.
- Hand over a collated set, not loose sheets; colour-code sections if pages must be found quickly.
- Print only what most people will use; add a shareable PDF where the rules allow.
- The leave-behind must match the submitted proposal and add no new commitments.

## 4. Room: on the day, 45 to 60 minutes early

**Walk the room and check:**

- From the back row: sightlines and screen legibility; the screen high enough to see over heads.
- Microphone and sound tested before the panel arrives.
- Lights and blinds: enough light for notes without washing out the screen.
- Cables taped across walkways; exits known; occupancy limits respected.
- A clock visible to the presenter, not the panel.
- Temperature, ventilation and water; ask whether rain on an iron roof will drown speech, a real problem in some East African venues.
- A black or blank slide ready for moments when the screen is not needed.

**Presenter's bag:** laptop and charger; deck as PDF and native file on USB and on a second device; display adapters; clicker with spare batteries; extension lead and tape; printed speaker notes; printed handouts; markers; water; power bank; phone hotspot.

**Seating, when we control the room (workshops):** theatre style for large information sessions; classroom for note-taking; U-shape or boardroom for discussion; clusters of four to six for group work. Confirm capacity with the venue and local fire limits.

## 5. Delivery

- Stand beside the screen; the panel should see you first and the slide second.
- Say more than the slide; talk only about what is shown, or blank the screen.
- Look across the whole panel; do not settle on one friendly face.
- Pause after important points; vary pace and emphasis.
- Natural gestures; no gripping the lectern.
- Humour only if it comes naturally and never at anyone's expense; true stories work better than jokes.
- If equipment fails, adapt calmly: "The projector has its own view today; let me take you through the handout instead."

## 6. Meetings the proposal promises (inception, steering, workshops)

- Call a meeting only when a message, call or document will not do; handle personnel matters one to one.
- Send the agenda one to two days ahead: objective, time-boxed items, a named lead for each, the expected output (decision, list, plan).
- During: follow the agenda, keep to time, repeat each question before answering, close with the decisions and the next meeting date.
- After: minutes and action log (owner, task, date) within two working days; follow up actions; check whether the meeting met its objective.
- Meeting cost rule of thumb: hourly cost ≈ twice the sum of attendees' hourly pay; use it to argue for fewer, shorter, decision-focused meetings.
- East African practice: budget time for opening protocol; government meetings may need formal invitation letters and adopt minutes at the next meeting; circulate minutes as a PDF plus a short text summary where WhatsApp is the working channel.

Agenda line pattern: "[hh:mm–hh:mm] · [action verb + topic] · Lead: [name] · Output: [decision / list / plan]."
