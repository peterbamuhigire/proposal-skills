## 1. Account intelligence: what the client will actually act on

A client's published strategy (for boards, investors, funders and regulators) and the operational wants of the people running the business this quarter are rarely the same. What the client actually implements sits between the two; Marcos et al. call this its "pragmatic strategy". Annual reports alone will not reveal it.

**Account-intelligence procedure (engine sequence)**

| Step | Action | Record |
|---|---|---|
| Read | Annual report, strategic plan, funder or investor statements, regulator filings, recent tenders | Source and date for every point |
| Listen | Interviews at several levels, including operators and front-line managers | Who said what, and when |
| Trace the pain | For each problem raised, follow the chain from surface symptom to operational, financial and strategic cost (the pain-chain habit used in the SaaS discovery skills) | Cost in the client's own measures |
| Force trade-offs | Ask respondents repeatedly to pick the most and least important item from small sets (quality, speed, cost, support); watch how users actually work and ask them to compare situations, to surface needs they cannot put into words | Ranked priorities; unspoken needs |
| Converge | Where stakeholders disagree, raise it tactfully and help them agree before we propose (see the token exercise below) | Agreed joint priorities |
| Translate | Turn findings into a short list of goals we can deliver, one opportunity at a time | Goal list for the account plan |

**Question bank, grouped by what the account plan needs**

- *Targets:* What must be achieved this year and in three to five years? Which targets drive bonuses and board attention?
- *Pressures:* Which competitors, regulators or funders shape their moves? Where are they gaining or losing ground?
- *Decisions:* Who decides, who holds the budget, how are purchases approved, which procurement route applies?
- *Problems:* What goes wrong today, how often, and what does it cost?
- *Horizon:* What will they need that they cannot yet describe? Which risks could we help them avoid before they are seen? (A supplier adds most value by helping a client avoid problems it has not yet noticed.)

**Token exercise for priority convergence:** give each client stakeholder ten tokens to spread across three to five priorities (quality, delivery time, cost, risk, local presence). Show the combined distribution, discuss the differences and let the client agree its joint priorities before it tells us what it needs.

**Planned and emergent moves:** record both the deliberate plan and the emergent moves visible in day-to-day decisions (a distinction from Mintzberg), and revisit the account plan when they diverge. If the client moves into areas where we cannot add value, say so rather than drift.

## 2. Decision-making unit (buying group) roles

| Role | Description |
|---|---|
| Initiator | Raises the need |
| User | Works with the result |
| Influencer | Shapes the specification or shortlist |
| Gatekeeper | Controls access and information (often procurement or an executive assistant) |
| Decider | Makes the final choice |
| Controller | Holds the budget |
| Buyer | Runs the purchase process and contract |

One person can hold several roles. In public procurement the evaluation committee, accounting officer and contracts committee have formal roles; map them from the procurement rules.

## 3. GRASP: one row per buying-group member

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| **G**oal | What we want to achieve with this person, and our next action |
| **R**ole | Their decision-unit role(s) |
| **A**ppeal | What matters to them about the offer and the buying process |
| **S**tate | The current state of our relationship (unknown, cold, neutral, warm, advocate; note any past incident) |
| **P**ower | Level and source of their influence (formal authority, expertise, budget, access) |

**How to fill the map (engine procedure):**

1. List everyone who touched the last purchase or will touch this one, from the procurement record, meeting notes and organisation chart.
2. Assign decision-unit roles first; a person with no role is removed from the map.
3. For each remaining person, write the Appeal in their language, not ours, and record where it came from (a meeting, an email, a public statement).
4. Grade the State honestly, including any past incident, and date the grade.
5. Score Power by source (formal authority, budget, expertise, access) rather than by title.
6. Write the Goal last: what we need from this person next, the action, the owner and the date.

**Illustration (fictional, East African SACCO union):** the chief executive is decider and values member growth for the annual general meeting; relationship neutral; high formal power. The ICT officer is influencer and user, cares about USSD uptime and integration effort, and is warm after a pilot. The board treasurer is controller, wants predictable costs in shillings, has not met us, and holds budget power. The procurement committee secretary is gatekeeper and buyer, wants a compliant file and no surprises, and knows us only from the last tender.

**Multi-threading check:** for each account, count active relationships by level (executive, director, manager, operator) and function. Flag any account with a single thread or with no senior tie. Plan named owners on our side for each important client contact.

## 4. Analysing the client's business

**Nine-box customer SWOT:** start from the client's strategic objectives, list its strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats, then derive four strategy types we can support:

| | Opportunities | Threats |
|---|---|---|
| **Strengths** | Attack: use strengths to seize opportunities | Defend: use strengths to counter threats |
| **Weaknesses** | Convert: fix weaknesses to seize opportunities | Block: limit exposure where weakness meets threat |

Prioritise the few moves where our offer helps, then express them as value hypotheses.

**Competitive profile matrix (our position against rival suppliers):** list the client's critical success factors, weight them to sum to 1.0, rate each supplier 1 to 4 on each factor with evidence, and sum weighted scores. Use the result to choose where to compete and what to concede.

**Financial diagnosis in the client's language (confirm formulas with the finance engine):**

- Revenue index by segment (base year = 100) shows which lines grow.
- Cash conversion cycle = inventory days + receivable days − payable days; working capital released ≈ days reduced × (annual revenue ÷ 365) for the receivables- or inventory-driven share.
- Operating margin = operating income ÷ revenue.
- Return on capital employed = operating income ÷ (total assets − current liabilities).

Illustration (fictional): a manufacturer with UGX 36 billion revenue a year (about UGX 99 million a day) that cuts its cash cycle by 20 days releases roughly UGX 2 billion of working capital. Present that figure to the finance director, not "better supply-chain visibility".

## 5. Voice of the customer for key accounts

Design a feedback programme: set objectives, choose channels (structured interviews, short targeted surveys, site observation, advisory group), feed findings to the account and delivery teams, keep confidentiality, and always tell the client what changed: "In March you told us [feedback]. Since then we have [change], and you will see it in [deliverable]."
