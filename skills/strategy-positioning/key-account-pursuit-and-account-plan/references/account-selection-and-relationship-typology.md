# Account Selection and Relationship Typology

Parent skill: [Key-Account Pursuit and Account Plan](../SKILL.md).

**When to read:** at workflow steps 2 and 5, when deciding which accounts deserve key-account investment and when setting a relationship target.

Sources (methods only, paraphrased): McDonald's key-account classification and the relationship-quality and trust models as presented in Marcos, J., Guesalaga, R., Hough, A. and Vincent, R. (c. 2025) *The High-Performing Key Account Manager*, Kogan Page (imprint year to verify); client grading, captive universes and complaint economics from Hunter, V. L. with Tietyen, D. (1997) *Business to Business Marketing: Creating a Community of Customers*, NTC Business Books. Statistics in those books are not used; several in the first are unsourced and are excluded.

## 1. Key-account selection

A key account is chosen for strategic importance, not size. Score each candidate on two axes, with evidence:

| Axis | Evidence to use |
|---|---|
| Profit-growth potential over three to five years | Client growth plans, budget lines, share of wallet we do not yet hold, margin history |
| Our strength with the account | Relationships by level, delivery record, incumbency, reference value, switching cost |

| Type | Potential | Our strength | Management approach | Lead profile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strategic | High | High | Joint annual plan, co-created value, sponsor, multi-function contact | Senior business manager who plans with the client |
| Star | High | Low | Time-boxed pursuit budget, entry proposition, relationship building | Entrepreneurial pursuit lead |
| Status | Low | High | Delivery excellence, margin protection, efficient service | Disciplined delivery manager |
| Streamline | Low | Low | Productised, low-touch tier or managed exit | Service desk or account coordinator |

Add three modifiers to the score: strategic importance (opinion leader, reference, market access), willingness to run innovative projects, and relationship strength per stakeholder (in owner-managed East African groups, access to the owner or MD often decides strength).

**Rules**

- Review the classification annually and remove key status when circumstances change.
- Keep a list of "next flagship" candidates.
- Record revenue concentration. Set a firm-specific threshold for dependence on one client and name a diversification action when it is crossed; no universal percentage is prescribed.

## 2. Small, knowable universes (captive-universe pursuit)

When the whole target market is small (licensed commercial banks, insurers, SACCOs above a size threshold, private hospitals, international schools, registered manufacturers), treat prospects like existing clients:

1. Build the universe list from regulator registers and association member lists; record the source and date of each list.
2. Qualify each organisation against the fit criteria; drop poor fits.
3. Run a named-account contact plan: useful insight at a steady cadence, events, introductions, then proposals.
4. Respect data-protection rules: use business contact details, record lawful basis, honour objections to direct marketing (Uganda s.26 objection right, claim PL-01; Kenya absolute objection right and free opt-out, claim PL-02).

## 3. Client grading for contact economics

For a portfolio of existing clients, grade by **expected** value, not last year's sales, and match contact cost to value:

1. Remove house accounts (bespoke plans) and very small occasional buyers (low-cost, reactive service).
2. Split the rest into five grades by count (a working split: AA top 5%, A 15%, B 25%, C 25%, D 30%).
3. Set a target contact-cost-to-revenue ratio per grade (firm-specific).
4. Start from the contact the client says it prefers; move content from visits to calls to digital updates until each grade's cost fits its ratio.
5. Promote under-served accounts that look like higher grades for a trial period.

Lower grades receive fewer but denser contacts. Every contact must carry something the client values.

## 4. Relationship typology: where the account sits now and where it should go

Two dimensions: dependence (supplier-dependent, mutual, client-dependent) and exchange style (transactional or relational).

| Position | Description | Typical action |
|---|---|---|
| Vulnerable supplier | We depend on them; transactional | Broaden our portfolio, raise switching value, invite a more relational mode |
| Vulnerable client | They depend on us; transactional | Avoid exploiting it; move to fair, relational terms |
| Inviting client | We depend on them; they choose to collaborate | Protect the goodwill; build mutual investment |
| Inviting supplier | They depend on us; we choose to collaborate | Decide whether to invest in mutual dependence |
| Competitive interaction | Mutual dependence; transactional (annual tender) | Build joint measures and planning |
| Collaborative partnership | Mutual dependence; relational | Maintain with joint planning and shared investment |

Set a target position, a timeline and dated actions. Typical paths: vulnerable towards inviting towards collaborative; competitive towards collaborative.

## 5. Relationship quality

Relationship quality = managed conflict + trust + commitment.

- **Conflict:** separate functional debate about how to reach shared goals from dysfunctional conflict (misaligned expectations, pricing disputes, contradictory messages from sales and delivery, perceived neglect).
- **Trust:** credibility (competence, reliability, honesty) plus goodwill. Where several suppliers are equally credible, goodwill decides loyalty.
- **Commitment:** relationship-specific investment and willingness to accept short-term cost.

Four drivers to act on: shared goals and measures; proactive information sharing (including early disclosure of problems); reciprocity and shared upside; personal ties earned through reliability.

**Relationship-health survey:** ask both sides the same short set of agree-disagree statements on the four drivers (for example "We share information relevant to each other's plans"; "When they help us, we reciprocate"). Compare the two views; the gaps are the agenda.

## 6. Trust development and recovery

**Five ways trust develops:** calculative (clear contracts, specific investments), predictive (repeated reliable behaviour), capability (credentials, case studies), intentional (acts beyond the contract that show goodwill), transferred (endorsements and trusted referrers).

**Trust equation (Maister et al., *The Trusted Advisor*):** trustworthiness rises with credibility, reliability and intimacy, and falls with self-orientation. Lower self-orientation by recommending the lighter option when it fits and by saying where we fall short.

**Trust-recovery protocol after a delivery failure**

1. Acknowledge within one working day and take ownership.
2. State the impact on the client in the client's terms.
3. Issue a resolution plan with steps, owners and dates.
4. Send progress updates until closed.
5. Share a root-cause review and the process fix.
6. Rebuild through consistent follow-through.

Script: "We missed [commitment]. The impact on you was [impact]. Here is what we are doing, who owns it, and when you will next hear from us."

## 7. Relationship-cycle checks (what destroys relationships)

| Stage | What the client needs | Behaviour to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| First contact | A real need, an offer that fits | Wrong target, self-focus, over-promising |
| Pursuit | Less uncertainty, sight of a fair deal | Prescribing before diagnosing, mixed messages |
| Onboarding | Affirmation, feeling valued | Not thanking, not confirming priorities, no second-step offer |
| Growth | Proactivity, added value | Treating the client like a prospect, single-line focus |
| Recovery | Quick response, being heard, fair treatment | Rationalising, offering a fix before listening |

**Loyalty segments for review:** secure (satisfied, would refer, share rising), complacent (somewhat satisfied, share flat), at risk (neutral, share flat at best), defector (would not refer, share falling). Measure behaviour (repeat, expand, refer), not satisfaction scores alone; ask identified clients "Would you work with us again?" and "Would you recommend us to a colleague?".
