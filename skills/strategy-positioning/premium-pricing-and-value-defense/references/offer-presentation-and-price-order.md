# Offer Presentation and Price Order

Parent skill: [Premium Pricing and Value Defense](../SKILL.md). Related: [proposal phrase bank](../../../writing-content/premium-commercial-writing/references/proposal-phrase-bank.md) sections 11 to 14.

**When to read:** when designing commercial options, presenting price in a proposal or meeting, building agency or retainer offers, or defending a fee against a cheaper bid. Procurement templates and separate-envelope rules always take precedence over the presentation order below.

## 1. Price order in conversation and in private-sector proposals

Four steps: (1) identify the problem; (2) show what it costs the client; (3) show how it is solved; (4) only then state the price.

**Exception: pre-disqualification.** When size signals suggest the client cannot afford the work, state the range early to save both sides time: "Our programmes for work of this kind run from [UGX X] to [UGX Y] a month. Is that within range for you right now?" This is courteous, not presumptuous.

**Price timing by offer type**

- Premium, judgement-heavy work: build value before price, but never make price hard to find once the reader reaches the cost section.
- Commodity or highly comparable services: show price early to meet expectations.
- Where price is expected (the financial proposal, an investment page, a rate card): state it plainly and immediately.
- Discounts: always show the original price, the new price and the saving together.

## 2. Cost paragraph order (inside the cost section)

Price → what the client achieves for it → what is included beyond that → why the fit makes price secondary. Write every proposal as if a cheaper bid will be on the evaluator's desk: restate needs fit, extras and expertise **next to** the price.

## 3. Options tables (proposal option tables, rate cards)

- Show two to four options per group; never a single option, because clients decide by comparison.
- Group like with like; separate unrelated service lines (build, retainer, audit).
- Align rows so features appear in the same order and wording across columns.
- Highlight one option ("Recommended" or "Most chosen") and only if the label is true.
- Place the option you most want to recommend where it will be compared fairly; state the reason for the recommendation.
- Decide the order deliberately (highest first sets a high reference; lowest first maximises uptake) and treat it as a test, not a rule.
- Offer a guarantee, pilot or stage gate near the price where it reduces risk.
- Put a contact route (phone, WhatsApp) beside the table in proposals that are read by owners.
- Options differ in scope, support or pace, never in the quality controls that protect the result.
- Choice overload evidence is mixed; keep options few because it aids comparison, not because of a single famous study.

## 4. Deliverables ordering as price defence

List the deliverable the client values most first, then extras as priced options. If the price is challenged, the client can remove extras without cutting the core. Never quietly downscope: if the client's expectation cannot be met at any acceptable price, say so before submitting.

## 5. Agency and retainer offer design

Use for marketing, digital-marketing, website care and advisory retainers (see also [marketing and digital services proposals](../../premium-client-proposal-strategy/references/marketing-and-digital-services-proposals.md)).

- **Programmes, not menus:** sell a small number of defined programmes (no more than three per service line) rather than à-la-carte builds.
- **Fast signal plus slow compounding:** pair every slow-building component (search visibility, content authority, brand) with a fast-signal component (a paid campaign, an enquiry flow, a quick-win fix) so the client sees evidence before the third or fourth month, when doubts about return typically surface.
- **Wrap the build inside the programme:** for website-led agencies, present the website build as the set-up phase of a recurring care or growth programme, or pair every project with an optional care retainer. A strict "no projects" rule does not suit web-design work.
- **Entry offers must lead somewhere:** an entry offer (audit, diagnostic, direction boards) is a paid, diagnostic path into the core programme, never a cheap version of it.
- **Engagement architecture for marketing work:** customer insight report (fixed fee) → strategic plan (fixed fee) → test sprint (fixed fee plus test media) → monthly optimisation retainer. This protects the client and the agency from "just run ads" on a broken funnel.
- **Fee floor:** if the fee a programme needs to be done properly cannot be charged, the problem solved is probably too small, the proof too weak, or the scope wrong. Rework the offer rather than underprice it.
- **Revenue paths check (for the agency's own plan):** tabulate how an annual revenue target could be met at four to six fee levels (clients needed, monthly wins after churn, meetings needed at the expected close rate) and choose a fee level the lead-generation engine can feed. Route the agency's own business plan to `business-plan-skills`.

## 6. Cost-to-fulfil check (before quoting a retainer)

List each deliverable, its hard cost and its basis (one-off set-up, monthly, annual); total set-up cost and monthly cost-to-serve; then set the price for a margin that allows growth. East African lines to include: mobile-money merchant fees, withholding tax and VAT treatment (confirm with the finance engine; do not state rates from memory), data bundles and connectivity, power backup, courier for physical items, and foreign-currency software subscriptions with exchange-rate risk. Uganda VAT on non-resident digital services and DST mechanics are finance-engine territory (claim PL-05, partial).

## 7. Defending against a cheaper bid

1. Establish whether the client is treating the service as a commodity; if so, the value difference has not been shown.
2. Make the difference concrete: inputs, method, assurance, outcome and risk of failure.
3. Show what the cheaper choice costs later (rework, delay, lost revenue, internal management time).
4. Restate the value against the price.

Language: "A cheaper option is possible. What it usually leaves out is [the element the client said it needs]. Which matters more this year: the lower fee, or [that outcome]?"

## 8. Growth economics for retainer cases

When arguing for a retainer or account investment, expanding an existing client's scope usually moves lifetime value more than reducing acquisition cost. Show both sensitivities with the firm's own numbers (contribution per year × expected life − acquisition cost), labelled as estimates.

## 9. Closing the commercial conversation

- "Let me send you a proposal" is not a close. Before the call ends, agree the decision date and book the proposal walk-through.
- The follow-up document is the programme overview, the agreement and the payment arrangement, not a new pitch.
- Use a choice close: "These are the two options. Which do you prefer?"
- If no decision: agree a decide-by date and send the overview, agreement and two relevant client stories the same day.
